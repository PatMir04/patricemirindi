The world in numbers, late 2026: five signals from the newest World Bank data

November 20, 2026 · 3 min read Lire en français

The World Bank updated its World Development Indicators on 11 November 2026. Working from the same 15 indicators as this site's dashboard, five signals stand out: steady growth, a more connected world, wider financial inclusion, slow poverty progress — and one series that has quietly stopped in 2023.

Every few months, quietly, the world's most-used development dataset changes. On 11 November 2026 the World Bank published its latest World Development Indicators update, adding 2025 values for many headline series. I track 15 of those indicators on this site's dashboard, across 32 countries and economies, and I re-check them every week. Here is what the newest vintage says. All figures below are world aggregates from that update.

One caution before the numbers: each indicator keeps its own calendar. Some series already reach 2025, others stop in 2024 because they rest on household surveys, and one, as we will see, has not moved since 2023. The latest year is part of the story.

Growth held steady

The world economy grew 2.92% in 2025, and GDP per capita reached $14,406. Inflation held near 3% in 2025, at 3.04%, exports held at 29.5% of GDP, and unemployment sat at 4.79%. None of these numbers describes a boom, but together they describe an expansion that is still intact: output rising, price pressures steady, and labour markets holding. For programme teams, this is the backdrop against which country-level results should be read — a country growing more slowly than 2.9% in 2025 was losing ground against the world average.

A more connected world

73.6% of the world's people used the internet in 2025 — roughly three in four. Access to electricity reached 91.9% in 2024, its latest year. Connectivity is no longer a niche concern for digital programmes; it is close to a default condition in most places where development teams work. The remaining gaps are increasingly concentrated: the roughly 8% of people still without electricity, and the quarter of the world not yet online, are the hardest to reach, and averages will flatter any programme that does not look for them specifically.

Financial inclusion kept climbing

78.7% of adults worldwide owned an account in 2024, the latest Global Findex reading. As covered in my earlier article on the Findex 2025, the recent story is not just access but use — saving, payments and resilience. Two practical notes for analysts: this series is survey-based, so it appears only in survey years (2011, 2014, 2017, 2021, 2024), and a dashboard that seems to show "no change" between those years is showing the survey calendar, not stagnation.

Poverty is moving slowly

The share of people living in extreme poverty was 10.1% in 2024, the latest available year. Life expectancy reached 73.5 years in 2024, and world population passed 8.22 billion in 2025. Poverty estimates rest on household surveys that take time to collect and process, so the world learns its poverty rate roughly two years late. Decisions made this year are therefore being taken with a poverty picture from 2024 — worth remembering whenever a single headline figure is asked to carry more weight than it can bear.

The signal in the gaps

The most useful finding in this update may be an absence. The world cereal yield series — a basic food-security indicator — still ends in 2023, at 4,229 kg per hectare, even after the November 2026 update. Agriculture was 4.01% of world GDP in 2025 and still employs 25.8% of workers, yet one of its simplest productivity measures is running three years behind. Dashboards rarely announce a stale series; the chart simply stops, or worse, quietly repeats. The habit that protects you is unglamorous: check the latest year of every indicator you publish, every time you publish. It is why the 15 indicators behind this site's dashboard get re-verified weekly, and why the dashboard shows its source and update date next to the charts.

You can explore all 15 indicators, for 32 countries and economies, on the development indicators dashboard — and if your team publishes numbers that other people rely on, I am glad to help you build the checking habit into your own reporting. Start a conversation.
